What this measures

Holiday Spend asks a language model to estimate what two people will spend per day in a city it has not seen before. The model returns ten prices in US dollars, called anchors: an inexpensive meal, a mid-range meal for two, a beer, a coffee, a cocktail, a glass of wine, a hostel dorm bed, a private hostel room, a basic (1-star) hotel room and a 3-star hotel room. The app turns these anchors into daily budgets for each accommodation and drinks tier, converts them to Australian dollars and scales them for the number of travellers.

This page tracks a hill climb: a series of controlled experiments that each change one thing about how those ten prices are produced, scored against independently collected real prices, and kept only if they make the estimates measurably more accurate.

How error is measured

For each anchor that has a reference price, the error is the size of the logarithm of the ratio between the estimate and the reference:

anchor error = | ln(estimate ÷ reference) |

Logarithms treat overestimates and underestimates evenly in proportion: twice the price and half the price both score 0.69. They also make a $2 error on a $4 coffee count as much as a $50 error on a $100 hotel room, since both are 50% off.

Worked example. A model estimates a 3-star room at $60 a night; the reference is $50. Error = |ln(60 ÷ 50)| = ln 1.2 = 0.182, about 20% on the right-hand axis. Had it estimated $42, the error would be |ln(42 ÷ 50)| = 0.174, about 16% too low but shown as ≈19%, because the percentage axis converts back as eerror − 1.

Anchors are then combined into one score per city, weighted by how much each item contributes to a typical mid-range day for two, so the score follows the money a traveller actually spends:

The weights are rescaled over the anchors a city has references for. Cocktail and wine have no reliable reference source yet, so in practice the score covers the other eight. If a model fails to return a price, that anchor scores ln 4 (1.39, a fourfold miss), so a method cannot look better by dropping hard answers.

The panel error plotted is the average city score across the panel's development cities. Each city counts once, so large, well-documented cities do not dominate.

Why a log error?

  • Prices differ by multiples, not amounts. A dorm bed is $8 in Hanoi and $60 in Zurich. Being $5 out is a disaster for the first and excellent for the second. A ratio, and so a log, measures what matters: how many times too high or too low.
  • It is fair to both directions. The common percentage error (|estimate − reference| ÷ reference) caps an underestimate at 100% but lets an overestimate grow without limit, so it quietly rewards guessing low. On the log scale, double and half are the same distance from the truth.
  • It puts every item on one scale. A coffee and a hotel room, in any currency, can be averaged together because the log error has no units.
  • It matches how the corrections work. A correction multiplies a price by a factor. On the log scale, multiplying becomes adding, so the best factor is simply the average log gap between references and estimates.
  • Absolute rather than squared. Averaging the absolute value, not the square, stops a handful of very wrong cities from dominating the score, and keeps the number readable as a typical percentage.

Reference prices and panels

Reference prices are real prices collected from independent sources, never from the models being tested. A panel is one fixed set of cities and references. When the references improve, a new panel is built and the methods are rescored on it, rather than mixing old and new numbers.

R1 · 54 cities

The first panel: well-known cities. Food and drink from Numbeo's crowdsourced prices; hostels from Hostelworld; hotels from BudgetYourTrip's KAYAK budget and mid-range ranges.

R2 · 333 cities

R1 plus 279 smaller, harder cities from the app's own library, the kind of places users actually add. Food and drink use livingcost.org, adjusted to Numbeo's level, where Numbeo has no data.

R3 · booking hotel prices

R2 with hotel references replaced by actual prices: the median 3-star price on Booking.com and Trip.com (member prices including taxes), averaged across six stay dates from November to July for a typical-season level. The basic room is the cheaper quarter of decently rated hotels and guest houses. This follows the owner's decision that the 3-star tier should reflect what a 3-star room really costs to book.

Held-out test · 142 cities

Cities set aside before any experiment, by a fixed hash of the city name (30% of cities). No method was tuned on them, and every look at them is logged. See Held-out test.

Methods tried

Every method starts from the same production prompt (v1.1): the model returns the ten anchors from its general knowledge, plus a current exchange rate, and the app's code does all the arithmetic. Experiments then varied one thing at a time.

Correction factors

Models are biased in consistent ways: for example, they price 3-star hotels like a mid-range booking site rather than at what rooms really cost. A correction factor multiplies each anchor by the average ratio of reference to estimate, learnt from other cities. Versions tried: one factor per anchor; per anchor and world region; and per country, leaning on the region when a country has few cities.

Each city's factor is learnt only from other cities (leave-one-city-out), so a city never helps correct itself. In the app the factors would be fixed numbers learnt once from all panel cities.

Prompt wording

Clearer item definitions, saying prices should be current, answering in local currency, a local example per item, prices of three comparable cities, and stricter accommodation definitions. Raw gains from wording mostly shifted prices up or down, which the correction factors already fix, so after correction none improved enough to keep.

Models and effort

Claude Haiku 5.5 was used for cheap screening; GPT-6 Luna at maximum reasoning is the production model. Haiku at high effort matched maximum effort at a fraction of the cost. Luna plus region correction beat Haiku and became the incumbent. Averaging Luna and Haiku helped too little to justify two calls.

Web search

Letting the model search for all prices helped food and drink but made accommodation worse. Restricting search to restaurant, coffee and drink prices (up to five searches, plus one for the exchange rate) kept the food gain without harming hotels.

Bundle 022 (current best)

Three changes that each helped a little, judged together as one package that was registered before its results existed: Luna, web search for food and drink only, and country-level correction factors. It cut the error from 0.205 to 0.163 on R3, and held up on the held-out cities.

How a change is accepted

A candidate method is compared city by city with the current best on the same cities. To separate real improvement from luck, the comparison is resampled 10,000 times (a paired bootstrap), giving a 95% range for the improvement. A change is accepted only if all of these hold:

  • the average error falls by at least 0.015 (about 1.5 percentage points), so small gains that would not pay for extra cost or complexity are not adopted;
  • the whole 95% range is an improvement (its lower end is above zero);
  • no single anchor gets worse by more than 0.05;
  • the share of failed or invalid answers rises by no more than 2 points;
  • at least 90% of cities have a score under both methods.

The rule was fixed before the experiments and has not been changed. Prompt changes are judged after their own correction factors are refitted, so a change that merely moves all prices up or down earns no credit. Some rejected points sit below the line because their gain was real but smaller than 0.015.

Held-out test

Choosing the best of many experiments on the same cities can flatter the winner. To check, the final method and the previous best were each scored once on the 142 held-out cities, using a scoring plan written down before any answers were collected and correction factors learnt only from development cities.

On R3 the bundle scored 0.190 against 0.220, an improvement of 0.031 (95% range 0.016 to 0.046). The gain held on cities no experiment had seen. Almost all of it is food and drink; hotel accuracy on held-out cities was about the same for both methods.

Limits

  • Food and drink references come from Numbeo, which the model's searches can also read. Part of the search gain may be the model finding the same source it is scored against, so it may overstate accuracy against what travellers really pay.
  • Hotel prices depend on the definition. Booking-site 3-star prices sit about 40% below KAYAK's mid-range category. R3 follows actual 3-star booking prices; the corrections learn whichever level is chosen.
  • Reference prices are noisy. Booking.com and Trip.com disagree by about 13% for a typical city, so errors well below that cannot be distinguished from reference noise.
  • No reference yet for cocktails or wine, so those anchors are not scored.
  • Corrections fix level, not ranking. They remove consistent over- or underestimates by item, region and country. What remains is how well the model ranks cities within a region.

Glossary

Anchor
One of the ten prices the model returns. Every daily budget tier in the app is calculated from them.
Reference price
An independently collected real price for an anchor in a city, used as the answer key.
Panel
A fixed set of cities with their reference prices. Methods are only compared on the same panel.
Development and test cities
Development cities are used to compare methods and learn corrections. Test cities are set aside and scored only to confirm the final choice.
Raw
The model's prices exactly as returned, before any correction.
Correction factor
A multiplier learnt from other cities that removes a model's consistent over- or underestimate for one anchor in one region or country (also called calibration).
Leave-one-city-out
Learning a city's correction only from the other cities, so the score reflects how the correction performs on a city it has not seen.
Shrinkage
Pulling a factor learnt from a few cities towards a broader average (country towards region), so a single unusual city cannot set it.
Geometric mean
The average of logs, converted back. It is the right average for prices: the geometric mean of $50 and $200 is $100, halfway in ratio terms.
Incumbent
The best accepted method so far, which every new candidate must beat.
Paired bootstrap
Resampling the cities many times and comparing two methods on the same draws, to see how much their difference could be luck.
95% range
The range that holds the improvement in 95% of resamples (a confidence interval). If it excludes zero, the improvement is unlikely to be chance.
Bundle
Several small changes judged together as one package, registered before results were seen, so they cannot be cherry-picked afterwards.
Lane
The model and settings that answer a set of prompts: Haiku for cheap screening, Luna (the production model) for decisions.
Budget weight
How much an anchor counts in the city score, in proportion to its share of a typical mid-range day for two.
